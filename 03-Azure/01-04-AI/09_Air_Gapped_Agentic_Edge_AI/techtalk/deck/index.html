Let's zoom into the biggest item. When you install Foundry Local as an Arc extension, it syncs a catalog into the cluster: 177 models, as a ConfigMap. You pick one by alias in a ModelDeployment. [12:30, 2 minutes] I let the chart axes draw, then the pen plots the recorded readings one by one.

Before we seal anything, one design choice that makes the whole thing work on a CPU. The simulator publishes a reading every five seconds. These are real ticks from the hack's recordings.

The dashed line is the dataflow's threshold. In Azure IoT Operations, a filter that evaluates to true drops the message. So the expression says: temperature at or below 85 and vibration at or below 7.5, drop it. (I wait while the normal readings fall away.) Six of these nine never reach the agent. Only three cross the threshold and land on the anomalies topic.

[click] Why does this matter? Because each one that passes costs a real inference on CPU. In the recording, about 8.7 seconds each. You do not want to spend that on a reading that says everything is fine.

So: filter at the broker, and think only when it matters.

[14:30, 1 minute 30 seconds] I let the room's walls draw, then each door opens with its rule.

Now the seal itself. In the lab, the line is a Kubernetes NetworkPolicy called enforce-airgap-sovereign-mode, on the edge-agents namespace. Read it as a room. It selects every pod in the namespace and says: egress is denied unless a rule allows it.

There are four doors, and all of them face inward. One, DNS to kube-system on port 53. Two, the MQTT broker on 1883 and 18883. Three, the Foundry Local and Istio namespaces on 80, 443 and 8080. Four, the namespace itself. (I point at the left wall.) And toward the internet: no door. No rule means denied.

[click] One door people get wrong is the first one. Why would a policy that's all in-cluster need DNS? Because once any egress policy selects a pod, everything else is denied, DNS included. Leave out rule one and your agent can't even resolve the broker's name. That's one of the grounding questions in challenge five.

So: allow-list inward. Outward has no door.

One honest caveat before you start. In the lab, our line is a NetworkPolicy on one namespace, in an Azure VM. That proves the agent's loop needs nothing outside. It doesn't make the VM a disconnected site.

In a real plant, the host is Azure Local hardware or a rugged K3s box, and the site has no route out from day zero. The workloads don't change: the hack's Readme says the same manifests run unchanged on Azure Local.

[click] What changes is how things cross. For a permanently disconnected site, Azure Local Disconnected Operations and the Foundry Local Expansion Pack bring Istio, the Gateway API CRDs, cert-manager and the model artifacts into a local registry. That's our transfer manifest, grown up: a release process that says what crosses, who checks it, and when.
