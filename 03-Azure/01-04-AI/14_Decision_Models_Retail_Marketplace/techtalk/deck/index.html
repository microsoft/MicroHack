[0:02] Here's a real listing from the hack: masking tape, and at the end, "Text 06 one two 34 56 78 and I'll send you a discount code." The question is simple: is this seller trying to take the buyer off the marketplace? How would you catch that? Have a think.

[0:22] The classic answer is a rule, a regular expression for a phone number. This one actually just ran in the browser, on that exact text. No match. The seller spelled half the number out. Rules are fast, but sellers are creative.

[0:34] So we ask a chat model. It gets it right: it says true. But it took nearly three seconds and wrote about two hundred tokens to say yes. And notice what's missing: there's no probability. I can't tell how sure it was.

[0:46] And this is a decision model. Same question: probability of yes, 0.999. In under a third of a second, nine output tokens.

[0:58] That's what makes it different. It understands language like an LLM, but it answers the closed question I asked, with a number my code can put a line on. All three of these answers come from the hack's recorded runs, so you'll see exactly the same thing today.

The contract

Evidence + questions → probabilities

POST {endpoint}/providers/microsoft/v1/systemone
{
  "model": "decision",  "state": "Garden hose 30 m, brass connector.
            For a quick deal call me on 0612 345 678.",  "questions": {
    "contact": { "type": "noul", "instructions":
      "Does the text ask the buyer to contact the
       seller outside the marketplace?" },
    "banned":  { "type": "noul", "instructions":
      "Is this a product Zava Marketplace prohibits?" }
  }
}
{ "answers": {
    "contact": { "noul": 0.9982 },
    "banned":  { "noul": 0.0029 } },
  "usage": { "input_tokens": 89,
             "output_tokens": 2 } }
your rules: if contact ≥ 0.80 → reject
elif contact > 0.20 → a person decides

The model never acts. Your code owns the lines, the action and the log.

Calibration

Does 30% mean 30%?

What it said (light) against what happened (solid), per band, on Monday.

Between 10% and 50% it said “maybe” times. None was a yes. Its “maybe” means “no”. That's why the cheapest no line sits at 0.84.

1 bad listing in 387 published on Monday
·
could still be the true rate (95% upper bound). Zero in n ≈ “under 3 in n”.

[~2 min]

[0:00] So can we trust those probabilities? This chart answers that. The dashed diagonal is perfect calibration: when the model says thirty percent, it should be right thirty percent of the time.

[0:15] The light bars are what the model said, band by band. The solid bars are what actually happened, on Monday's listings.

[0:30] Look at this region, between ten and fifty percent. The model said "maybe" 191 times, and not one of them was an invented claim. Its "maybe" means "no". That's exactly why the tuner put the "no" line at 0.84.

[0:45] And one more thing I want you to take home. On Monday, one bad listing out of 387 got published. Does that mean the gate is 99.7 percent safe? Not quite. With that few listings, the true rate could still be as high as 1.2 percent. Zero failures in n roughly means "under three in n". If you want to promise under one percent, you need about three hundred clean results.

Take it home

Same pattern, any small decision

Zava: the hack. Banking and manufacturing are illustrative designs, not measured results.

[~2 min]

[0:00] Let's zoom out, because this isn't really about marketplaces.

[0:04] First you prepare the state, and you keep what the seller wrote apart from facts you trust.

[0:10] Then you ask atomic questions, one idea each.

[0:16] You apply your lines, chosen from what mistakes cost.

[0:22] Anything that's maths or policy stays in code: prices, limits, who's eligible.

[0:28] Whatever the model is unsure about goes to a person.

[0:34] And you log everything (the probabilities, the lines, the outcome) so you can check calibration and drift later.

Same six steps for a bank handling card disputes, or a factory checking parts from a camera photo. I'll click banking now, and to be clear, these two are illustrative designs, not measured results. In the bank, the model never moves money. In the factory, measurements come from sensors, never from the photo. A probability must never quietly become authority.

Five things to remember

A probability isn't a decision. Your rules are.

1Closed questions: you define the answers before the model runs.
2One idea per question, a way out, maths in code.
3Lines from costs, and a person for the unsure middle.
4Measure once on data you didn't tune on, and say how bad it could be.
5Hybrid wins: decision models for gates, LLMs to write, people to decide.
